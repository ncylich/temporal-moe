\title{Temporal Mixture-of-Experts:\\ Serving Memory that Scales with Active Parameters}

\begin{abstract}\normalsize
Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models improve language model quality by activating only a small fraction
of parameters per token, but serving them still requires the full expert pool in memory. We study
\emph{temporal MoE}, which enforces expert reuse across neighboring tokens so serving memory can
scale with active rather than total parameters. Unlike post-hoc caching or offloading, we impose
locality as a training-time constraint. A rolling-residency router keeps only the $k$ active experts
of each layer resident and swaps at most one expert per token. Across FLAME-MoE isoFLOP sweeps
spanning $10^{16}$ to $10^{19}$ FLOPs, temporal MoE recovers 72--82\% of the MoE-over-dense
quality gain at compute-optimal sizes, and zero-shot downstream accuracy at $10^{19}$ tracks the
recovery. The fine-grained configuration holds 18 of 192 experts resident, a 5.7$\times$ cut in
whole-model weight memory. A llama.cpp implementation serves an $\sim$11B-scale model at 0.83$\times$
the all-resident decode speed in 5.1$\times$ less VRAM. The trained
router internalizes the constraint. Expert specialization shifts from token identity to
surrounding context, and temporal self-consistency nearly doubles over vanilla routing.
Trained-in locality makes MoE-quality models practical to serve in the memory of their active
parameters.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Sparse MoE layers buy quality with total parameters while paying compute only for the $k$ experts
active per token \cite{shazeer2017,dai2024deepseekmoe,jiang2024mixtral}. Serving inverts the
bargain. In FLAME-MoE-1.7B \cite{kang2025flame}, routed experts hold 91\% of all weights, yet every
expert must sit in fast memory even though most do nothing for a given token. Offloading systems
stream experts from slower storage on demand \cite{eliseev2023,skliar2024}, but per-token offload
is bandwidth-infeasible. Hiding one expert miss costs roughly the fast-to-slow bandwidth ratio,
20--110 resident-expert computations on modern hardware, while only $k\!\approx\!6$--$8$ experts of
work exist per layer to hide it behind. Caching lowers the miss rate, not the miss cost, and
vanilla routers give it little to work with, as consecutive tokens share only about 20\% of their
expert choices \cite{jiang2024mixtral,muennighoff2024olmoe,zhu2025}. The locality that serving
needs must be trained in, not recovered after the fact.

We contribute (i) a rolling-residency MoE trained from scratch under a hard residency constraint
with an unmodified loss stack, (ii) isoFLOP evidence at $10^{16}$, $10^{17}$, $10^{18}$, and $10^{19}$ FLOPs
that it recovers most of the MoE quality gain at a fraction of the resident memory, and that
zero-shot task accuracy at $10^{19}$ tracks the pretraining recovery, (iii) a mechanistic account
of how the router internalizes the constraint, a shift of expert selection off token identity and
onto context (Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}), together with measured negative results that redirect
future work from cache policy toward anticipation, and (iv) a real-engine llama.cpp serving
measurement that validates the memory-latency claim.

\section{Method}\label{sec:method}

Each MoE layer with $E$ routed experts keeps only its $k$ active experts resident, the set $S_t$.
Given router logits $R_t\in\mathbb{R}^E$ for token $t$, the layer nominates the best
non-resident expert, $n_t=\arg\max_{e\notin S_{t-1}}R_t[e]$, and swaps it in only if it beats the
worst resident, which is \mbox{evicted},
\begin{equation}
S_t=
\begin{cases}
\bigl(S_{t-1}\setminus\{\arg\min_{e\in S_{t-1}}R_t[e]\}\bigr)\cup\{n_t\}, & R_t[n_t]>\min_{e\in S_{t-1}}R_t[e],\\[1pt]
S_{t-1}, & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
The token is served by $S_t$ with gates from the softmax of
resident logits, and $S_0$ cold-fills from the first token's top-$k$. Training uses the standard language
modeling, load-balancing, and z-losses unchanged. The swap is non-differentiable, but the router
receives ordinary gate gradients on every resident it scores, so the function the swap rule reads
is trained normally. The selection scan runs as a single-launch Triton kernel and costs about 1\,ms
per layer call, under 1\% of step time at our scales.

The single-swap budget is what lets the load hide behind compute. At small batch the expert FFN is
memory-bandwidth-bound, so serving the $k-1$ already-resident experts reads $(k-1)\,b$ bytes from
fast memory while the incoming expert streams its $b$ bytes from slower storage, and the load is
fully masked when $b/\mathrm{BW}_{\mathrm{slow}}\le(k-1)\,b/\mathrm{BW}_{\mathrm{fast}}$, that
is, once $k-1$ approaches the fast-to-slow bandwidth ratio (roughly 32 for RAM over NVMe). The
expert size $b$ cancels, so the condition depends only on the routing width, which DeepSeek-style
fine-graining toward $k\!\approx\!32$ reaches without changing active parameters or FLOPs. With 18
of 192 experts resident (the same fraction as 6 of 64 in the coarse model) the routed-expert
footprint shrinks 10.7$\times$ and whole-model resident memory 5.7$\times$. Section~\ref{sec:results}
tests these calculations in a real engine, including a variant that computes the router earlier
in the network than its experts to lengthen the masking window.

We build on FLAME-MoE \cite{kang2025flame} (64 experts, top-6, one shared expert) in two versions,
a coarse-expert model (6 active of 64 experts) and a fine-grained model (18 of 192, DeepSeek-style
splitting \cite{dai2024deepseekmoe}). At $10^{16}$ and $10^{17}$ FLOPs we sweep isoFLOP curves over
0.77M--15.1M active non-embedding parameters with a 16k vocabulary. At $10^{18}$ we sweep three
sizes around an exact FLAME-MoE-38M-100M replication (4.45B tokens, 50k pythia vocabulary), and at
$10^{19}$ we train the budget's compute-optimal shape and evaluate it zero-shot on six standard
tasks with lm-evaluation-harness. For serving, we implement rolling residency in a llama.cpp CUDA
fork and benchmark batch-1 prefill and decode on Qwen3-MoE-shaped random-weight models ($\sim$11.3B total,
Q4\_K\_M, both granularities scaled $\sim$10$\times$), with $R=k$ expert slots held in VRAM
against a full CPU pool and at most one swap per layer per token. Prefill runs expert-major, and each
needed expert streams once through a fixed ring of slots and serves all of its tokens in one
batched pass. Weights are irrelevant to a latency and memory measurement. All runs use
locked hyperparameters and report test-set
bits-per-byte (BPB, tokenizer-invariant, seed noise $\approx$0.003). Each shape gets a dense floor
matched to the MoE's active parameters and FLOPs, and we define \emph{recovery} as
$(\text{dense}-\text{temporal})/(\text{dense}-\text{MoE})$.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\textbf{IsoFLOP bands.} At every model size in the three budgets with full size sweeps
(Figure~\ref{fig:isoflop}), temporal MoE sits well inside the dense-to-MoE band and recovers
72--82\% of the MoE-over-dense gain at compute-optimal sizes (72\% and 77\% for the fine-grained
model, 82\% coarse) with only $k$ experts ever resident. The isoFLOP parabola and its minimum match the dense and
MoE baselines at every budget, so the constraint does not distort scaling geometry.

\textbf{Larger budgets.} At $10^{18}$ FLOPs (Figure~\ref{fig:isoflop}, bottom left) recovery is 73\%
against the strongest full MoE, consistent with the smaller budgets. Sometimes the
mechanism appears nearly lossless, here outperforming
its equally fine-grained MoE counterpart (1.335 vs.\ 1.346 BPB). At the coarse granularity, with
two seeds per arm, temporal holds parity with the strongest full MoE (seed ranges 1.311--1.313
vs.\ 1.316--1.320 BPB). Fine-graining itself hurts the full
MoE at this scale (at 48.5M active it lands within 0.008 BPB of the dense floor) while temporal
routing is robust to it, so the only fine-grained model we take to $10^{19}$ is the temporal one.
At $10^{19}$ (Figure~\ref{fig:isoflop}, bottom right), our largest budget, the temporal models
keep 78--81\% of the MoE-over-dense gain and the granularity preference flips: the fine-grained
temporal model (1.065 BPB) now edges out the coarse one (1.068).

\textbf{Serving.} Table~\ref{tab:serving} closes the loop in a real engine. The deploy row
serves the fine-grained model at 0.83$\times$ the all-resident decode speed in 5.1$\times$ less
VRAM. The measured 5.1$\times$ is the end-to-end counterpart of the 5.7$\times$ weight
arithmetic in Section~\ref{sec:method}, and the gap is the KV cache and workspace buffers, which
sit in VRAM regardless of residency. The other rows isolate two costs. Kernel overhead is granularity-independent
(0.86--0.88$\times$). The streaming cost is set by granularity, roughly free for fine experts
(840\,KiB per swap, hidden behind compute) but dominant for coarse ones (2.5\,MiB, 0.51$\times$).
The asymmetry is the measured converse of the $10^{19}$ crossover. Scale pushes temporal quality
toward fine granularity, and fine granularity is what streams cheaply, so quality and serving
select the same design point. The router-early variant lengthens the masking window and recovers
most of the fine model's remaining gap, as the bandwidth analysis anticipates. Figure~\ref{fig:servingctx}
extends the measurement to prefill and long context. Prefilling 16k tokens takes 1.6$\times$
the all-resident time and decode holds near 0.80$\times$ throughout. Peak VRAM stays under
3.6\,GiB against 8.0--9.3\,GiB all-resident, a gap the growing KV cache compresses from
3.7$\times$ at 1k to 2.6$\times$ at 16k. At larger batch each sequence carries its own resident
set, so the advantage narrows there too (protocol and kernel details in
Appendix~\ref{app:serving}).

\textbf{The constraint is internalized.} Figure~\ref{fig:locality} shows why this works. A router
trained under residency shows temporally banded \emph{unconstrained} preferences, and its
next-token self-consistency nearly doubles over vanilla routing at every scale probed. The effect
is streaming, not collapse. Over a 2048-token sequence a temporal model still touches 83--98\% of
its expert pool (96--100\% at $10^{19}$), and no expert is resident more than 40\% of the time
(25\% at $10^{19}$), so quality comes from time-multiplexed access to the full pool rather than a
static subset.

\section{De-lexicalization of routing}\label{sec:mechanism}

Section~\ref{sec:results} showed that the router internalizes the constraint. This section asks
what it learns instead of the free routing it can no longer express. We analyze trained temporal
and full-MoE pairs at the two budgets whose full artifacts are preserved, fine-grained (18 of
192) at $10^{16}$ and coarse (6 of 64) at $10^{17}$ FLOPs. At these budgets the full MoE holds a
small quality edge, so what follows characterizes what the constraint changes, not whether it
helps. Protocols and full tables are in Appendix~\ref{app:mechanism}.

\textbf{Usage concentration.} We measure each expert's usage breadth as the effective fraction
of evaluation tokens it serves, the normalized inverse Simpson concentration of its routing
mass, which is 1 under uniform usage and near 0 when usage concentrates on a few recurring
tokens. In the fine-grained pair, the median full-MoE expert serves an effective 30\% of the
token stream and almost no expert serves a majority of it. The median temporal expert serves
66\%, two thirds of temporal experts serve a majority, and the coarse pair shifts the same way.
Expert weights, in contrast, are nearly unaffected, equally distinct and near orthogonal in both
regimes. Independent seeds and a sigmoid-router control reproduce every gap
(Appendix~\ref{app:mechanism}). The constraint
therefore changes how experts are used rather than what they compute, and the rest of this
section identifies what replaces token ownership.

\textbf{Token to context.} For each expert, two logistic probes predict its firing, one from the
current token's embedding alone and one from the surrounding window with the token excluded,
sized to one residency lifetime, the number of active experts. Figure~\ref{fig:delex} (left) plots every expert.
Full-MoE routing is close to a token lookup. The token alone predicts firing at median
AUC 0.93, and context dominates for under 1\% of experts. Temporal routing inverts the picture.
Token AUC falls to 0.62, and context dominates for 85--91\% of experts. We call this
\emph{de-lexicalization}. The shift is visible forward in time: a probe predicting the
router's next-token expert selection from the preceding tokens' routing record alone reaches
AUC 0.85 on temporal models against 0.64 for the full MoE at matched granularity, because
context persists across neighboring tokens and token identity does not. A data-weighted logit
lens \cite{logitlens} agrees on the output side, where full-MoE experts promote coherent word
clusters and temporal experts show no detectable vocabulary preference.

\textbf{Constraint strength.} Although residency restricts the router relative to its
unconstrained preferences, each model performs best under its training regime: unmasking
a temporal model and imposing residency on a full MoE both degrade quality
(Table~\ref{tab:swap}), so the routing organization is trained into the weights rather than
removable at inference. The constraint's strength is also a continuous dial.
Decoupling the cache size $R$ from the active $k$ interpolates between our deployed setting
($R = k$) and the full-MoE recipe ($R = E$) at identical FLOPs, and held-out BPB falls
monotonically as the cache grows (Figure~\ref{fig:delex}, right), so at this budget the
constraint is a pure, tunable quality cost. $R$ is the number of experts held in fast memory, so
the curve is the serving memory-quality frontier. The maximal constraint costs +0.023 BPB at
roughly a tenth of the routed-expert memory.

A free router binds experts to token identities. Rolling residency makes that binding
unrepresentable while leaving weights untouched, so the router reorganizes around context, which
persists across a residency lifetime. That reorganization explains how quality survives the
constraint, the near-doubled self-consistency of Figure~\ref{fig:locality}, and the forecastable
demand that the anticipation direction of Section~\ref{sec:outlook} targets.

\section{Conclusion and next steps}\label{sec:outlook}

Across four compute decades, two tokenizer families, and two expert granularities, training under
rolling residency keeps 72--82\% of the MoE-over-dense quality gain with only the active experts
resident. It preserves the baselines' scaling geometry, and the recovery carries to downstream
tasks at $10^{19}$. A real-engine implementation serves the fine-grained model at 0.83$\times$
the all-resident decode speed in 5.1$\times$ less VRAM. Quality survives because the router
reorganizes. Routing de-lexicalizes, and experts bind to context that persists across a residency
lifetime (Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}).

Our negative results narrow down where the remaining deficit can be closed
(Appendix~\ref{app:negatives}). A coherence loss that imitates past routing hurts at every
weight. Better eviction is worth little, while a future-demand oracle gains 18--30 points of hit
rate. Two objectives that reward the router's own predictability both Goodhart (one pins an
expert, the other collapses diversity). The headroom is anticipation, and it must be decoupled
from the router's objective.

Two directions follow. The first is to adapt a released MoE checkpoint to residency with
router-only training (zero-shot imposition is the costliest direction in Table~\ref{tab:swap}),
which would make temporal routing a serving recipe for existing MoE-class models in the memory
of their active parameters. The second is to establish the architecture's
stability profile, weight and activation norms against the baselines, loss stability, and
behavior under reinforcement learning, where routing flips between the rollout engine and the
trainer are the identified collapse driver \cite{ma2025r3,zheng2025gspo}, so the recipe is
viable end to end with no caveats. Residency cuts both ways there. The resident set is extra
state the trainer must replay, but the single-swap budget bounds routing drift to one expert per
layer per token, a bound vanilla routing does not have.

The end goal is local inference. If temporal routing holds at scale, a Kimi-K2-scale sparse model
\cite{kimik2} could run in the memory of its active parameters on quantized consumer hardware.
That would put near-state-of-the-art models within private, local reach of the average person.

\section{De-lexicalization details}\label{app:mechanism}

All statistics in Section~\ref{sec:mechanism} derive from router probabilities, expert weight
matrices, and expert output vectors on a fixed evaluation batch of 64 sequences of 2048 tokens
per model.

\textbf{Selectivity and weight identity.} Renormalizing expert $e$'s gate mass over token
positions gives $q_e(t)$, and selectivity is the normalized inverse Simpson concentration
$1/(N\sum_t q_e(t)^2)$, the effective fraction of the stream feeding the expert. Generalists are
experts above 0.5, and router flatness is per-token entropy normalized by $\ln E$. Weight
identity flattens each expert's FFN weights and reports cosine distance to the expert-weight
centroid and median pairwise cosine. Table~\ref{tab:structural} gives all cells. Independent
seed replicates agree to about 0.01 in selectivity and two points in generalist fraction, and
the regime gaps are ten to forty times larger.

\textbf{Firing probes.} For each expert, the label marks whether it is in the token's top-$k$.
Two logistic classifiers predict the label from the model's own input embeddings, the current
token alone versus the mean embedding of tokens within window $w$ with the current token
excluded. The exclusion prevents context features from encoding the token itself. Classifiers
fit on the first 70\% of tokens and report AUC on the last 30\%. Refitting on null labels, both
iid permutations and circular shifts of at least 1000 tokens (preserving residency-induced
autocorrelation), gives $0.500 \pm 0.002$ everywhere. At $w = k$, median token AUC / context AUC
/ fraction context-dominated are 0.93 / 0.64 / 1\% (full MoE, 192 experts), 0.62 / 0.77 / 91\%
(temporal, 192), 0.84 / 0.59 / 1\% (full MoE, 64), and 0.60 / 0.68 / 85\% (temporal, 64),
consistent at every MoE layer (89--92\% context-dominated by depth for the fine temporal model).
Across $w \in \{k/2,\, k,\, 32\}$ the fine pair stays context-dominated (88--94\%), and the
coarse pair is context-dominated at both residency-scale windows, washing out only at $w = 32$,
five times its cache lifetime. The demand-prediction probe is causal and history-only: features
at token $t$ are the current gate values and a short demand history (lags $t$ to $t{-}3$ and
fast and slow exponential averages), with no hidden states, and the label is whether an expert
is in the router's selection at the next token. AUC is 0.85--0.87 across temporal models and
0.64 for the full MoE at matched granularity.

\textbf{Output lens.} Averaging each expert's output vectors over the tokens actually routed to
it and projecting through the final norm and unembedding gives the vocabulary distribution the
expert promotes. Its exponentiated entropy is an effective vocabulary between 1 and the 16k
vocabulary size. Projecting without data conditioning reads about 15{,}990 for every expert, the
method's no-signal reference. Full-MoE experts reach median 14{,}612 with a sharpest decile of
9{,}683, whose top promoted tokens read as coherent lexical clusters. Temporal experts sit at
median 15{,}932, with no word-list experts even in the tail.

\section{Negative results}\label{app:negatives}

\textbf{Coherence loss.} An explicit loss that pulls router logits toward the current resident
set hurts monotonically at every weight scanned at $10^{16}$, across both granularities. The
constraint is already internalized, and a backward-looking gradient adds no new information.

\textbf{Eviction replay.} Replaying logged routing through alternative eviction shows the
offline-optimal (Belady) policy is worth only 6--10 points of set hit rate over our least-logit
rule, and LRU is 4--8 points worse, while an oracle that selects swaps by short-horizon
\emph{future} demand gains 18--30 points. Cache policy is nearly saturated. Anticipation is not.

\textbf{Predictability objectives.} Two objectives that reward the router's own predictability
both Goodhart at sweep budgets. Auxiliary-loss-free, DeepSeek-inspired selection biases
\cite{wang2024auxfree} with demand momentum raise residency alignment (+7 points of next-token
overlap at the best swept setting) but induce a de-facto pinned expert, resident 85\% of the
time, with no quality gain. An anticipatory loss on discounted future demand raises alignment
sharply (+26 points) by teaching the router to concentrate demand so its own future is easy to
predict. Streamed expert diversity collapses threefold (expert union 158 to 50 of 192) and BPB
worsens. A published loss of this family, CoSMoEs' block-wise expert selection
\cite{huber2025cosmoes}, reports the same trade, fewer expert replacements for a consistent
quality regression.
