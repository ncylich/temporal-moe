\title{Temporal Mixture-of-Experts:\\ Serving Memory that Scales with Active Parameters}

\begin{abstract}\normalsize
Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models improve language model quality by activating only a small fraction
of parameters per token, but serving them still requires the full expert pool in memory. We study
\emph{temporal MoE}, which enforces expert reuse across neighboring tokens so serving memory can
scale with active rather than total parameters. Unlike post-hoc caching or offloading, we impose
locality as a training-time constraint. A rolling-residency router keeps only the $k$ active experts
of each layer resident and swaps at most one expert per token. Across FLAME-MoE isoFLOP sweeps
spanning $10^{16}$ to $10^{19}$ FLOPs, our temporal MoE recovers 72--82\% of the MoE-over-dense
quality uplift at compute-optimal sizes with 18 of 192 experts resident, a 5.7$\times$ reduction in
resident memory, and zero-shot downstream accuracy at $10^{19}$ tracks this recovery. The trained router internalizes the constraint, shifting expert specialization
from token identity to surrounding context and nearly doubling its temporal self-consistency
over vanilla routing, with recovery stable across the whole range. Trained
temporal routing is a practical path toward MoE-quality models under
memory-constrained serving.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Sparse MoE layers buy quality with total parameters while paying compute only for the $k$ experts
active per token \cite{shazeer2017,dai2024deepseekmoe,jiang2024mixtral}. Serving inverts the
bargain. In FLAME-MoE-1.7B \cite{kang2025flame}, routed experts hold 91\% of all weights, yet every
expert must sit in fast memory even though most do nothing for a given token. Offloading systems
stream experts from slower storage on demand \cite{eliseev2023,skliar2024}, but per-token offload
is bandwidth-infeasible. Hiding one expert miss costs roughly the fast-to-slow bandwidth ratio,
20--110 resident-expert computations on modern hardware, while only $k\!\approx\!6$--$8$ experts of
work exist per layer to hide it behind. Caching lowers the miss rate, not the miss cost, and
vanilla routers give it little to work with, as consecutive tokens share only about 20\% of their
expert choices \cite{jiang2024mixtral,muennighoff2024olmoe,zhu2025}. The locality that serving
needs will not be found after training. It must be trained in.

We contribute (i) a rolling-residency MoE trained from scratch under a hard residency constraint
with an unmodified loss stack, (ii) isoFLOP evidence at $10^{16}$, $10^{17}$, $10^{18}$, and $10^{19}$ FLOPs
that it recovers most of the MoE quality gain at a fraction of the resident memory, with zero-shot
task accuracy at $10^{19}$ tracking the pretraining recovery, and (iii) a
mechanistic account of how the router internalizes the constraint, de-lexicalizing routing
from token identity to context (Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}), with two measured negative
results redirecting future work from cache policy toward anticipation.

\section{Method}

Each MoE layer with $E$ routed experts keeps only its $k$ active experts resident, the set $S_t$.
Given router logits $R_t\in\mathbb{R}^E$ for token $t$, the layer nominates the best
non-resident expert, $n_t=\arg\max_{e\notin S_{t-1}}R_t[e]$, and swaps it in only if it beats the
worst resident, which is \mbox{evicted},
\begin{equation}
S_t=
\begin{cases}
\bigl(S_{t-1}\setminus\{\arg\min_{e\in S_{t-1}}R_t[e]\}\bigr)\cup\{n_t\}, & R_t[n_t]>\min_{e\in S_{t-1}}R_t[e],\\[1pt]
S_{t-1}, & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
The token is served by $S_t$ with gates from the softmax of
resident logits, and $S_0$ cold-fills from the first token's top-$k$. Training uses the standard language
modeling, load-balancing, and z-losses unchanged. The swap is non-differentiable, but the router
receives ordinary gate gradients on every resident it scores, so the function the swap rule reads
is trained normally. The selection scan runs as a single-launch Triton kernel, verified bit-exact
against a unit-tested reference, adding under 50\% to step time at our scales.

The single-swap budget is what lets the load hide behind compute. At small batch the expert FFN is
memory-bandwidth-bound, so serving the $k-1$ already-resident experts reads $(k-1)\,b$ bytes from
fast memory while the incoming expert streams its $b$ bytes from slower storage, and the load is
fully masked when $b/\mathrm{BW}_{\mathrm{slow}}\le(k-1)\,b/\mathrm{BW}_{\mathrm{fast}}$, that
is, once $k-1$ approaches the fast-to-slow bandwidth ratio (roughly 32 for RAM over NVMe). The
expert size $b$ cancels, so the condition depends only on the routing width, which DeepSeek-style
fine-graining toward $k\!\approx\!32$ reaches without changing active parameters or FLOPs. With 18
of 192 experts resident (the same fraction as 6 of 64 in the coarse model) the routed-expert
footprint shrinks 10.7$\times$ and whole-model resident memory 5.7$\times$. These calculations are theoretical. We need to also explore them in real
kernels. A direction is computing the router earlier in the network than its experts,
lengthening the masking window.

We build on FLAME-MoE \cite{kang2025flame} (64 experts, top-6, one shared expert) in two versions,
a coarse-expert model (6 active of 64 experts) and a fine-grained model (18 of 192, DeepSeek-style
splitting \cite{dai2024deepseekmoe}). At $10^{16}$ and $10^{17}$ FLOPs we sweep isoFLOP curves over
0.77M--15.1M active non-embedding parameters with a 16k vocabulary. At $10^{18}$ we sweep three
sizes around an exact FLAME-MoE-38M-100M replication (4.45B tokens, 50k pythia vocabulary), and at
$10^{19}$ train its compute-optimal shape and evaluate it zero-shot on six standard tasks with
lm-evaluation-harness. All runs use locked hyperparameters and report test-set
bits-per-byte (BPB, tokenizer-invariant, seed noise $\approx$0.003). Each shape gets a dense floor
matched to the MoE's active parameters and FLOPs, and we define \emph{recovery} as
$(\text{dense}-\text{temporal})/(\text{dense}-\text{MoE})$.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\textbf{IsoFLOP bands.} Across every model size and all three swept budgets
(Figure~\ref{fig:isoflop}), temporal MoE sits well inside the dense-to-MoE band, recovering
72--82\% of the MoE uplift at compute-optimal sizes (72\% and 77\% for the fine-grained model,
82\% coarse) with only $k$ experts ever resident. The isoFLOP parabola and its minimum match the dense and
MoE baselines at every budget, so the constraint does not distort scaling geometry.

\textbf{Paper scale.} At $10^{18}$ FLOPs (Figure~\ref{fig:isoflop}, bottom left) recovery is 73\%
against the strongest full MoE, consistent with the smaller budgets. In fact, sometimes the
mechanism appears nearly lossless, here outperforming
its equally fine-grained MoE counterpart (3.977 vs.\ 4.009 nats). At the coarse granularity, run
with two seeds per arm, it appears to surpass the strongest full MoE outright: every temporal
seed lands below every MoE seed (3.904--3.909 vs.\ 3.918--3.930 nats). Fine-graining itself hurts the full
MoE at this scale (at 48.5M active it lands within 0.024 nats of the dense floor) while temporal
routing is robust to it, so the fine cell we carry forward is temporal. At $10^{19}$
(Figure~\ref{fig:isoflop}, bottom right), our largest budget, temporal keeps 78--81\% of the
MoE-over-dense gain and the granularity preference flips: the fine-grained temporal model (1.065
BPB) now edges out the coarse one (1.068). Scale moves the temporal optimum toward exactly the
granularity that makes streaming cheap.

\textbf{Downstream tasks.} Zero-shot accuracy at $10^{19}$ (Table~\ref{tab:lmeval}) tracks the
pretraining picture. The full MoE lifts the dense average from 0.392 to 0.424 (0.059 to 0.091
above chance), and temporal keeps 86\% (coarse) and 76\% (fine) of that above-chance uplift,
beating the full MoE outright on WinoGrande and matching it on PIQA. Recovery transfers from
perplexity to task accuracy.

\textbf{The constraint is internalized.} Figure~\ref{fig:locality} shows why this works. Trained
under residency, the router's \emph{unconstrained} preferences become temporally banded, and its
next-token self-consistency nearly doubles over vanilla routing at every scale probed. The effect
is streaming, not collapse. Over a 2048-token sequence a temporal model still touches 83--98\% of
its expert pool (96--100\% at $10^{19}$), and no expert is resident more than 40\% of the time
(25\% at $10^{19}$), so quality comes from time-multiplexed access to the full pool rather than a
static subset.

\section{De-lexicalization of routing}\label{sec:mechanism}

Section~\ref{sec:results} showed that the router internalizes the constraint. This section asks
what it learns instead of the free routing it can no longer express, analyzing trained temporal
and full-MoE pairs at the two budgets where full artifacts are preserved, fine-grained (18 of
192) at $10^{16}$ and coarse (6 of 64) at $10^{17}$ FLOPs. At these budgets the full MoE holds a
small quality edge, so what follows characterizes what the constraint changes, not whether it
helps. Protocols and full tables are in Appendix~\ref{app:mechanism}.

\textbf{Usage concentration.} We measure each expert's usage breadth as the effective fraction
of evaluation tokens it serves, the normalized inverse Simpson concentration of its routing
mass, which is 1 under uniform usage and near 0 when usage concentrates on a few recurring
tokens. The median full-MoE expert serves an effective 30\% of the token stream and almost no
expert serves a majority of it. The median temporal expert serves 66\%, and two thirds of
temporal experts serve a majority. Expert weights are, in contrast, nearly unaffected, remaining
equally distinct and near orthogonal in both regimes, with independent seeds and a
sigmoid-router control reproducing every gap (Appendix~\ref{app:mechanism}). The constraint
therefore changes how experts are used rather than what they compute, and the rest of this
section identifies what replaces token ownership.

\textbf{Token to context.} For each expert, two logistic probes predict its firing, one from the
current token's embedding alone and one from the surrounding window with the token excluded,
sized to one residency lifetime, the number of active experts. Figure~\ref{fig:delex} (left) plots every expert.
Full-MoE routing is close to a token lookup, the token alone predicting firing at median
AUC 0.93 and context dominant for under 1\% of experts. Temporal routing inverts the picture,
token AUC falling to 0.62 and context dominating for 85--91\% of experts. We call this
\emph{de-lexicalization}. The shift is visible forward in time: a probe predicting the
router's next-token expert selection from the preceding tokens' routing record alone reaches
AUC 0.85 on temporal models against 0.64 for the full MoE at matched granularity, because
context persists across neighboring tokens and token identity does not. A data-weighted logit
lens \cite{logitlens} agrees on the output side, where full-MoE experts promote coherent word
clusters and temporal experts show no detectable vocabulary preference.

\textbf{Constraint strength.} Although residency restricts the router relative to its
unconstrained preferences, each model performs best under its training regime: unmasking
a temporal model and imposing residency on a full MoE both degrade quality
(Table~\ref{tab:swap}), so the routing organization is trained into the weights rather than
removable at inference. The constraint's strength is, moreover, a continuous dial.
Decoupling the cache size $R$ from the active $k$ interpolates between the shipped constraint
($R = k$) and the full-MoE recipe ($R = E$) at identical FLOPs, and held-out BPB falls
monotonically as the cache grows (Figure~\ref{fig:delex}, right), so at this budget the
constraint is a pure, tunable quality cost. Because $R$ is the number of experts held in fast
memory, RAM, the curve is the serving memory--quality frontier, the maximal constraint
costing +0.023 BPB at roughly a tenth of the routed-expert memory.

A free router binds experts to token identities. Rolling residency makes that binding
unrepresentable while leaving weights untouched. The router reorganizes around context, which
persists across a residency lifetime. That move explains how quality survives the constraint,
the near-doubled self-consistency of Figure~\ref{fig:locality}, and the forecastable demand the
anticipation directions below target.

\section{Conclusion and next steps}\label{sec:outlook}

Two early probes shape the roadmap. An explicit coherence loss pulling router logits toward the
resident set hurts monotonically at every weight scanned. The constraint is already internalized,
and a backward-looking gradient adds no new information. Replaying logged routing through
better eviction shows offline-optimal eviction is worth only +6--10 points of hit-rate over our
greedy rule (LRU is 4--8 points worse), while an oracle selecting swaps by short-horizon \emph{future}
demand gains +18--30 points. The headroom is anticipation, not cache policy, consistent with
de-lexicalized, forecastable routing (Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}).

The recovery deficit is therefore a stable, attackable 18--28\% at the sweep budgets (the coarse
model already closes it at $10^{18}$), and we
pursue it on two fronts: auxiliary-loss-free, DeepSeek-inspired routing that replaces
balancing losses with gradient-free selection biases \cite{wang2024auxfree}, and an anticipatory
training loss targeting discounted future expert demand, injecting new information rather than
imitating the past.
Beyond closing the gap, the highest-leverage direction is converting released MoE checkpoints
by router-only adaptation under residency (zero-shot imposition is the costliest direction in
Table~\ref{tab:swap}). If it matches pretraining, temporal routing becomes a recipe for serving
MoE-class models in the memory of their active parameters.

\section{Vision}

Overall, even though this technique is not yet perfected, its promising performance could lay the
groundwork for future local large language models. The ultimate goal of this work is to push for a
future where users can run extremely sparse models, imagine a Kimi-K2.X-scale model \cite{kimik2}
running at similar performance, completely locally on quantized consumer GPU hardware.
Near-state-of-the-art models will be accessible, privately and safely, to the average person.

\section{De-lexicalization details}\label{app:mechanism}

All statistics in Section~\ref{sec:mechanism} derive from router probabilities, expert weight
matrices, and expert output vectors on a fixed evaluation batch of 64 sequences of 2048 tokens
per model.

\textbf{Selectivity and weight identity.} Renormalizing expert $e$'s gate mass over token
positions gives $q_e(t)$, and selectivity is the normalized inverse Simpson concentration
$1/(N\sum_t q_e(t)^2)$, the effective fraction of the stream feeding the expert. Generalists are
experts above 0.5, and router flatness is per-token entropy normalized by $\ln E$. Weight
identity flattens each expert's FFN weights and reports cosine distance to the expert-weight
centroid and median pairwise cosine. Table~\ref{tab:structural} gives all cells. Independent
seed replicates agree to about 0.01 in selectivity and two points in generalist fraction, and
the regime gaps are ten to forty times larger.

\textbf{Firing probes.} For each expert, the label marks whether it is in the token's top-$k$.
Two logistic classifiers predict the label from the model's own input embeddings, the current
token alone versus the mean embedding of tokens within window $w$ with the current token
excluded. The exclusion prevents context features from encoding the token itself. Classifiers
fit on the first 70\% of tokens and report AUC on the last 30\%. Refitting on null labels, both
iid permutations and circular shifts of at least 1000 tokens (preserving residency-induced
autocorrelation), gives $0.500 \pm 0.002$ everywhere. At $w = k$, median token AUC / context AUC
/ fraction context-dominated are 0.93 / 0.64 / 1\% (full MoE, 192 experts), 0.62 / 0.77 / 91\%
(temporal, 192), 0.84 / 0.59 / 1\% (full MoE, 64), and 0.60 / 0.68 / 85\% (temporal, 64),
consistent at every MoE layer (89--92\% context-dominated by depth for the fine temporal model).
Across $w \in \{k/2,\, k,\, 32\}$ the fine pair stays context-dominated (88--94\%), and the
coarse pair is context-dominated at both residency-scale windows, washing out only at $w = 32$,
five times its cache lifetime. The demand-prediction probe is causal and history-only: features
at token $t$ are the current gate values and a short demand history (lags $t$ to $t{-}3$ and
fast and slow exponential averages), with no hidden states, and the label is whether an expert
is in the router's selection at the next token. AUC is 0.85--0.87 across temporal models and
0.64 for the full MoE at matched granularity.

\textbf{Output lens.} Averaging each expert's output vectors over the tokens actually routed to
it and projecting through the final norm and unembedding gives the vocabulary distribution the
expert promotes. Its exponentiated entropy is an effective vocabulary between 1 and the 16k
vocabulary size. Projecting without data conditioning reads about 15{,}990 for every expert, the
method's no-signal reference. Full-MoE experts reach median 14{,}612 with a sharpest decile of
9{,}683, whose top promoted tokens read as coherent lexical clusters. Temporal experts sit at
median 15{,}932, with no word-list experts even in the tail.
