\begin{abstract}
Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models improve language model quality by activating only a small fraction
of parameters per token, but serving them still requires the full expert pool in memory. We study
\emph{temporal MoE}, which enforces expert reuse across neighboring tokens so serving memory can
scale with active rather than total parameters. Unlike post-hoc caching or offloading, locality is
imposed as a training-time constraint. A rolling-residency router keeps only the $k$ active experts
of each layer resident and swaps at most one expert per token. Across FLAME-MoE isoFLOP sweeps
spanning $10^{16}$ to $10^{18}$ FLOPs, temporal MoE recovers 72--82\% of the MoE-over-dense quality
uplift at compute-optimal sizes with 6 of 64 experts resident, a 5.7$\times$ reduction in resident
memory. The trained router internalizes the constraint, roughly doubling its temporal
self-consistency over vanilla routing, and recovery is stable across three orders of magnitude of
compute. Trained temporal routing is a practical path toward MoE-quality models under
memory-constrained serving.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Sparse MoE layers buy quality with total parameters while paying compute only for the $k$ experts
active per token \cite{shazeer2017,dai2024deepseekmoe,jiang2024mixtral}. Serving inverts the
bargain. In FLAME-MoE-1.7B \cite{kang2025flame}, routed experts hold 91\% of all weights, yet every
expert must sit in fast memory even though most do nothing for a given token. Offloading systems
stream experts from slower storage on demand \cite{eliseev2023,skliar2024}, but per-token offload
is bandwidth-infeasible. Hiding one expert miss costs roughly the fast-to-slow bandwidth ratio,
20--110 resident-expert computations on modern hardware, while only $k\!\approx\!6$--$8$ experts of
work exist per layer to hide it behind. Caching lowers the miss rate, not the miss cost, and
vanilla routers give it little to work with, as consecutive tokens share only about 20\% of their
expert choices \cite{jiang2024mixtral,muennighoff2024olmoe,zhu2025}. The locality serving needs
will not be found after training. It must be trained in.

We contribute (i) a rolling-residency MoE trained from scratch under a hard residency constraint
with an unmodified loss stack, (ii) isoFLOP evidence at $10^{16}$, $10^{17}$, and $10^{18}$ FLOPs
that it recovers most of the MoE quality gain at a fraction of the resident memory, and (iii) a
mechanistic account showing
the router internalizes the constraint, with two measured negative results that redirect future
work from cache policy toward anticipation.

\section{Method}

Each MoE layer with $E$ routed experts keeps only its $k$ active experts resident, a set $S_t$
with $|S_t|=k$. Given router logits $R_t\in\mathbb{R}^E$ for token $t$, the layer nominates the best
non-resident expert $n_t=\arg\max_{e\notin S_{t-1}}R_t[e]$ and swaps it in only if it beats the
worst resident, evicting that resident,
\begin{equation}
S_t=
\begin{cases}
\bigl(S_{t-1}\setminus\{\arg\min_{e\in S_{t-1}}R_t[e]\}\bigr)\cup\{n_t\}, & R_t[n_t]>\min_{e\in S_{t-1}}R_t[e],\\[1pt]
S_{t-1}, & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
At most one expert moves per token so that its transfer can hide behind compute. At small batch the
expert FFN is memory-bandwidth-bound, so serving the $k-1$ already-resident experts reads
$(k-1)\,b$ bytes from fast memory while the incoming expert streams its $b$ bytes from slower
storage, and the load is fully masked when
$b/\mathrm{BW}_{\mathrm{slow}}\le(k-1)\,b/\mathrm{BW}_{\mathrm{fast}}$, that is, once $k-1$
approaches the fast-to-slow bandwidth ratio (roughly 32 for RAM over NVMe). The expert size $b$
cancels, so the condition depends only on the routing width, which DeepSeek-style fine-graining
toward $k\!\approx\!32$ reaches without changing active parameters or FLOPs. We also plan to
explore computing the router earlier in the network than its experts' computation, lengthening the
window available to mask each load. The token is served by $S_t$ with gates from the softmax of resident
logits, and $S_0$ cold-fills from the first token's top-$k$. Training uses the standard language
modeling, load-balancing, and z-losses unchanged. The swap is non-differentiable, but the router
receives ordinary gate gradients on every resident it scores, which trains the scoring function the
swap rule reads. The selection scan runs as a single-launch Triton kernel, verified bit-exact
against a unit-tested reference, adding under 50\% to step time at our scales.

We build on FLAME-MoE \cite{kang2025flame} (64 experts, top-6, one shared expert) in two versions,
a coarse-expert model (6 active of 64 experts) and a fine-grained model (18 of 192, DeepSeek-style
splitting \cite{dai2024deepseekmoe}). At $10^{16}$ and $10^{17}$ FLOPs we sweep isoFLOP curves over model
sizes (0.77M--14.8M active non-embedding parameters) with locked hyperparameters, reporting
tokenizer-invariant bits-per-byte (BPB, seed noise $\approx$0.003). Each shape gets a dense floor
matched to the MoE's active parameters and FLOPs, and we define \emph{recovery} as
$(\text{dense}-\text{temporal})/(\text{dense}-\text{MoE})$. At $10^{18}$ FLOPs we replicate
FLAME-MoE-38M-100M exactly (4.45B tokens) and report validation cross-entropy. With 6 of 64 experts resident
the routed-expert footprint shrinks 10.7$\times$ and whole-model resident memory 5.7$\times$.

\section{Results}

\textbf{IsoFLOP bands.} Across every model size and both sweep budgets
(Figure~\ref{fig:isoflop}, left), temporal MoE sits well inside the dense-to-MoE band, recovering
72--82\% of the MoE uplift at compute-optimal sizes (82\% for the coarse model at both budgets,
72\% and 77\% fine-grained) with only $k$ experts ever resident. The isoFLOP parabola and its minimum match the dense and
MoE baselines, so the constraint does not distort scaling geometry.

\textbf{Paper scale.} At $10^{18}$ FLOPs (Figure~\ref{fig:isoflop}, right) recovery is 74\%
against the strongest full MoE, consistent with the smaller budgets across three orders of
magnitude of compute. In fact, sometimes the mechanism appears nearly lossless, here outperforming
its equally fine-grained MoE counterpart (3.977 vs.\ 4.009 nats).

\textbf{The constraint is internalized.} Figure~\ref{fig:locality} shows why this works. Trained
under residency, the router's \emph{unconstrained} preferences become temporally banded, and its
next-token self-consistency roughly doubles over vanilla routing at every scale probed. The effect
is streaming, not collapse. Over a 2048-token sequence a temporal model still touches 83--97\% of
its expert pool, and no expert is resident more than 39\% of the time, so quality comes from
time-multiplexed access to the full pool rather than a static subset.

\section{Outlook and next steps}

Two early probes shape the roadmap. An explicit coherence loss pulling router logits toward the
resident set hurts monotonically at every weight scanned; the constraint is already internalized,
and a backward-looking gradient adds nothing the router lacks. And replaying logged routing through
better eviction shows offline-optimal eviction is worth only +6--10 points of hit-rate over our
greedy rule (LRU is 7 points worse), while an oracle selecting swaps by short-horizon \emph{future}
demand gains +20--30 points. The headroom is anticipation, not cache policy.

The recovery deficit is therefore a stable, attackable 18--26\%, and we are pursuing it with
decision-time mechanisms, a swap margin on smoothed demand to spend fewer, better swaps, and an
anticipatory training loss whose targets are discounted future expert demand, new information
rather than imitation of the past. We are also weighing auxiliary-loss-free, DeepSeek-inspired
designs that replace routing losses with gradient-free selection biases \cite{wang2024auxfree}.
Beyond closing the gap, the highest-leverage directions are converting released MoE checkpoints by
router-only adaptation under the residency mask, and downstream task evaluation of the existing
checkpoint pairs. If either matches the pretraining results, temporal routing becomes a general
recipe for serving MoE-class models in the memory of their active parameters.

\section{Vision}

Overall, even though this technique is not yet perfected, its promising performance could lay the
groundwork for future local large language models. The ultimate goal of this work is to push for a
future where users can run extremely sparse models, imagine a Kimi-K2.X-scale model \cite{kimik2}
running at similar performance, completely locally on quantized consumer GPU hardware.
Near-state-of-the-art models will be accessible, privately and safely, to the average person.
